The strongest framing

The pitch is not “VR is definitely going to explode and you’ll make money.”

That will sound like salesmanship, and StartEngine itself stresses that these investments are speculative, illiquid, and can lose the entire principal. The more credible argument is: “This is risky, but if you already believe the VR ecosystem needs more ambitious games, this is a rare chance to help build the machinery that can repeatedly make them happen, while participating as a potential owner rather than simply pre-ordering one product.” That distinction should run through almost every answer.

The objections that actually matter

24 questions a skeptical player or investor may ask.

Not sanitized softballs. These are the questions that can stop someone from supporting the raise unless the answer feels credible.

01
“Why should the average VR player care about this?”
VR PLAYER
+

Because one of the biggest problems in VR isn't the hardware anymore. It's content. Players constantly ask why more of the games they already love aren't available in VR.

We exist specifically to close that gap.

More resources don't simply mean a bigger Flat2VR Studios. They mean more conversations with rights holders, more prototypes we can build before a publisher is willing to take the risk, more projects we can have in development at once, and more chances to turn a game you've wanted in VR for years into something real.

You don't have to invest to support that mission. Buying VR games, wishlisting them, reviewing them and telling friends matters enormously. But for people who want to go one step further, this is a way to potentially participate in the company trying to make more of those games happen.

02
“Why StartEngine instead of Kickstarter?”
VR PLAYER · CAPITAL
+

Because they solve two fundamentally different problems.

Kickstarter is fantastic when the goal is: “Help us make this specific thing.” A backer pledges money and might receive the game or another reward. Kickstarter specifically prohibits creators from offering equity or financial returns.

What we're trying to build here is bigger than one game.

We're building the infrastructure, team, technology, relationships and publishing capacity to bring many more games into VR over many years.

So this isn't: “Give us money so we can make Game X.”

It's closer to: “If you believe in what this company is building, here's an opportunity to potentially own a piece of that journey.”

Kickstarter can still make perfect sense for an individual project. StartEngine makes sense when the thing you're trying to grow is the company and its ability to repeatedly create projects.

04
“So what is the money actually going toward?”
CAPITAL · PROCESS
+

I would get much more concrete here than “growth.”

The basic answer should be:

More games. More capacity. More attempts.

That means expanding the development team so multiple adaptations can move forward simultaneously; funding prototypes and technical investigation before a project is guaranteed; supporting publishing opportunities through Impact Inked; investing in reusable VR technology, optimization and production pipelines; taking on platform work that makes ports financially viable; supporting QA, certification, production and launch; and giving the company enough runway to pursue projects that may take months or years of conversations before they become public.

Public descriptions of the campaign currently characterize the intended use as expanding the publishing portfolio and adding internal development capacity.

I'd eventually accompany this answer with a very simple diagram showing where $1 of investment roughly gets allocated once those numbers are finalized.

05
“Isn't investing in a VR company extremely risky?”
CAPITAL & RISK
+

Yes.

And we should never pretend otherwise.

This is an early-stage private investment. It can be illiquid for years, there may never be an exit, and you could lose your entire investment. StartEngine itself explicitly tells investors to assume these risks.

So nobody should invest money they need back.

But risk and impact aren't opposites.

The reason opportunities like this can matter is precisely because ambitious VR development is difficult to finance. If every project were obviously profitable and risk-free, the biggest publishers in the world would already be converting their entire catalogs.

The risk is real. So is the problem we're trying to solve.

For someone who already believes that more ambitious VR software needs to exist, this provides a way to back the infrastructure trying to make that happen while also potentially participating in the upside if the company succeeds.

06
“Are you basically asking fans to invest because traditional investors won't?”
CAPITAL & TRUST
+

No, and I wouldn't dodge this one.

Flat2VR didn't begin with a giant institutional check and a PowerPoint presentation. It grew out of people who were already making VR mods because they desperately wanted these experiences to exist.

Hartmann Capital is publicly listed as an investor in Impact Reality.

But there's something fitting about giving the VR community an opportunity to participate too.

Institutional capital asks: How large is the VR market today?

Our community often asks a different question: How large could it become if someone actually gave us the software we're asking for?

We think both perspectives deserve a seat at the table.

“If you've already raised money, why do you need more?”
CAPITAL
+

Because the constraint has increasingly become opportunity versus capacity.

That's a good problem to have, but it's still a problem.

There are games we can pursue, publishing opportunities we can support and partnerships we can build that require people, prototype budgets and runway before they generate revenue.

Raising additional capital isn't supposed to replace the business model. It's intended to accelerate it.

The question isn't simply, “Can Flat2VR keep existing?” It's “How much more could we do if capital wasn't the limiting factor?”

That doesn't remove business risk. But there's a very significant difference between investing in an idea and investing in a company that is already executing the model it's asking you to believe in.

09
“Why don't the owners of these huge IPs just make the VR versions themselves?”
BUSINESS MODEL
+

Because VR is still specialized.

A publisher may own an incredible game and still lack a team experienced in VR interaction, rendering constraints, comfort, platform certification, Quest optimization, PSVR2 features, motion controls and all of the thousand small decisions that separate “the game running in a headset” from a real VR adaptation.

That's part of the opportunity.

We don't need publishers to become VR studios.

We want to become the team they call when they want their game in VR.

Flat2VR has publicly described exactly this early challenge: IP owners were interested in VR but often didn't have their own path to making it happen, and the young studio first had to prove that major IP holders could trust it.

10
“Isn't porting an existing game much easier than actually making a game?”
BUSINESS MODEL · PROCESS
+

It removes one enormous category of risk: you already know what the game is.

That's an advantage, not something we'd apologize for.

We aren't spending years asking: Is this character fun? Does this gameplay loop work? Does anyone care about this universe?

But transforming that game into VR can be extremely difficult.

Interfaces may need to be completely rebuilt. Weapons become physical systems. Animation assumptions break. Cameras have to be reconsidered. Rendering budgets change drastically. A game built for a powerful PC may somehow need to run twice, at high frame rates, on a standalone headset.

Trombone Champ was an amusing example: Flat2VR deliberately started with a game that was effectively 2D and had to be reconceived as an immersive world.

We're trying to retain the benefit of starting with a proven game while taking on the specialized technical problem of transforming it into VR.

11
“How do you decide which games to pursue?”
PROCESS · BUSINESS MODEL
+

It starts with the same question that created Flat2VR in the first place:

Would people actually want to be inside this game?

Then comes the less glamorous part.

Can the gameplay translate well? Can we get the rights? Is there a business model that works for both sides? What platforms can support it? How technically difficult will the conversion be? How large is the likely audience? What would have to change? Can we create something worthy of the original game rather than simply checking a “VR supported” box?

We love VR enough to want everything in VR.

We also have to be disciplined enough to know that doesn't mean everything should become an official Flat2VR project.

12
“Okay, explain the actual Flat2VR process.”
PROCESS
+

I'd make this one of the centerpiece pieces of content.

A project often begins long before anyone hears about it. We identify games that we think could genuinely work in VR. We study the title technically and creatively. We approach the rights holder. Sometimes we demonstrate demand from the community. Sometimes we build something. Sometimes we spend months talking. We determine whether the economics can work. If we reach an agreement, the real development process begins: interaction design, VR mechanics, rendering, optimization, UI, comfort, platform work, QA, certification and publishing.

And then you finally see an announcement.

The announcement is the visible 5% of a process that may have been going on for a very long time.

13
“Why does it take so long to announce the games everyone is asking for?”
PROCESS
+

Because enthusiasm isn't a license agreement.

We can desperately want a particular game in VR and still need the IP owner to believe in the project, agree to acceptable economics, give us access to what we need technically and become comfortable trusting us with something valuable to them.

That's why building a track record is so important.

Every successful project doesn't merely create another VR game.

It makes the next conversation easier.

A new studio initially had to persuade companies that it could be trusted with their properties. Today, High on Life VR and System Shock VR are publicly in development.

That's the proof we want to keep building.

The most valuable thing we can accumulate isn't one particular license. It's a reputation among publishers that says:

“These are the people who know how to bring our game to VR.”

15
“What happens when a big pitch fails?”
PROCESS · CAPITAL
+

This is where I would absolutely tell stories.

People see the announcements.

They don't see the failures.

They don't see the flights, prototypes, decks, calls, negotiations and months of work that sometimes end with nothing being announced.

If you're legally/NDA-cleared to discuss the Dead by Daylight story, I'd say something almost exactly like:

We once flew to Montreal because we believed Dead by Daylight could be incredible in VR. We were serious enough about it to get on a plane and make the case. And it didn't become a Flat2VR game.

That's a side of this business people rarely see.

For every game we announce, there are conversations that don't work out. Sometimes the timing isn't right. Sometimes the economics don't work. Sometimes a publisher isn't ready. Sometimes we learn that the project itself isn't right.

But that's also why capacity matters. We can't build a company around assuming every pitch succeeds. We have to build a company capable of taking enough good swings that some of them do.

That story may sell the raise better than another success story.

It demonstrates persistence, shows where money actually gets spent before announcements happen, and makes clear why “more at-bats” matters.

16
“Does raising more money guarantee you'll get [GTA / Cyberpunk / BioShock / etc.]?”
CAPITAL · PROCESS
+

Absolutely not.

And we would never tell people otherwise.

Money cannot force an IP holder to sign a deal.

What it can do is give us the resources to approach more companies, create stronger pitches, build more prototypes, hire specialized people, withstand long negotiation cycles and be ready to move when an opportunity appears.

Capital doesn't guarantee the next yes. It lets us survive more no's on the way to it.

17
“What happens if one of your games flops?”
BUSINESS MODEL · CAPITAL
+

Then we learn from it and keep building.

That's another reason the company model matters.

If you crowdfund one game, almost the entire thesis rests on that game.

A studio and publishing portfolio can spread risk across multiple projects, genres, platforms and business models. One underperforming title can still create technology, relationships, porting knowledge and pipeline improvements that benefit later projects.

The goal isn't to never have a miss.

The goal is to build a company where one miss doesn't end the mission.

18
“What if VR remains niche?”
BUSINESS MODEL · CAPITAL
+

That's one of the real investment risks.

But our model is partially designed around that reality rather than pretending it doesn't exist.

Instead of requiring every project to justify the economics of creating an AAA game from scratch exclusively for today's VR audience, we can start with existing games, existing worlds, existing awareness and often existing technology.

That doesn't magically make every port profitable.

But it changes the economics of the problem.

We don't need VR to instantly become larger than traditional gaming for this model to make sense. We need to become very good at identifying projects where the cost of bringing something into VR can be justified by the audience available for it.

19
“Aren't you too dependent on Meta?”
BUSINESS MODEL
+

We don't want the future of Flat2VR tied to one headset manufacturer.

Our public slate already spans Meta Quest, SteamVR and PlayStation VR2.

And that's strategically important.

Different games will make sense on different hardware. Sometimes standalone will matter most. Sometimes preserving the scope or visual fidelity of a particular game may make PC or console more appropriate.

We're betting on VR, not on one company's headset.

20
“What happens if major publishers eventually start doing all these ports themselves?”
BUSINESS MODEL
+

That would be an excellent problem for us to have.

It would mean the category we spent years arguing for had finally proven itself.

And even then, publishers routinely outsource specialized development, porting, co-development and remastering work rather than maintaining every specialty internally.

Our long-term value isn't simply knowing how to put motion controls into a game.

It's relationships, technology, production experience, platform expertise, community knowledge and a track record of knowing which games translate well and how to translate them.

21
“Isn't this just taking advantage of passionate fans?”
CAPITAL · VR PLAYER
+

I would answer this unusually strongly:

If the only reason you're considering investing is because you love Flat2VR, don't invest.

Buy our games. Wishlist them. Review them. Tell people about them.

An investment is different.

It is speculative and you should only consider it after understanding the eventual terms and being comfortable losing the entire investment.

We want people supporting this because they understand what we're trying to build and believe the risk/reward makes sense for them, not because they feel obligated to help us.

Counterintuitively, I think that answer will increase trust substantially.

22
“Wouldn't I help VR more by spending $500 buying VR games?”
VR PLAYER · CAPITAL
+

Maybe.

And we'd be thrilled if that's what you choose to do.

Buying VR games is one of the most important things anyone can do to demonstrate that this market exists.

Investing is simply a different kind of participation.

Buying games supports what's available now.

Investing in a company like this is potentially helping finance what might be available later.

They're complementary, not competing forms of support.

23
“What exactly do I own if I reserve/invest?”
CAPITAL & TERMS
+

This one needs to be extremely precise.

At the current reservation stage, a reservation itself isn't an investment and doesn't mean you own anything. Public reporting on the current page describes it as an expression of interest where no money changes hands and neither side is committed.

If a formal offering follows, investors need to look at the actual offering documents to see the security being offered, valuation, price, voting rights, dilution provisions and other terms. StartEngine supports several structures, including common stock, preferred stock, convertible securities and debt, so you shouldn't describe the eventual ownership until those terms are actually set.

The bigger story

“Help Flat2VR grow” is generic startup language. “Help us make more attempts” is a story.

There are probably hundreds of games your community would lose its mind over if they became proper VR titles. The tragedy isn't that Flat2VR doesn't know those games exist. You know. The problem is all the invisible work between “Wouldn't this be amazing in VR?” and “Coming to Quest, SteamVR and PSVR2 next year.”

Show them the graveyard, not just the trophy case.

Rights. Relationships. Flights. Pitches. Prototypes. Engineering investigations. Deals that collapse. Deals that sit dormant for a year. Hardware limitations. Publishers who need convincing. Projects that initially appear impossible. Teams that don't exist yet. Money spent on things the public may never see.

That's what I would make the raise about.

Not: “Give us $X so we can hire more developers.”

But: “There are far more games we want to bring to VR than we currently have the capacity to pursue. We want to change that.”
